# Samādhi - The Concentration Manual

Concentration is a skill, and like any skill it has a method, characteristic obstacles, and recognizable milestones. This page covers the practical work: choosing an object, settling the mind on it, clearing what blocks it, and reading the signs that show the object is developing.

---

## Choosing an Object

The Visuddhimagga catalogs **forty meditation subjects** (_kammaṭṭhāna_), grouped by kind:

| Group                              | Count | Examples                                                                                   |
| :--------------------------------- | :---- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Kasiṇas** (devices)              | 10    | Earth, water, fire, air, four colors, light, space                                         |
| **Kinds of foulness** (_asubha_)   | 10    | Stages of a decaying corpse                                                                |
| **Recollections** (_anussati_)     | 10    | Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha, virtue, generosity, deities, death, body, breathing, peace         |
| **Divine abodes** (_brahmavihāra_) | 4     | Loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity                                   |
| **Formless states** (_arūpa_)      | 4     | Infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, neither-perception-nor-non-perception |
| **One perception**                 | 1     | Repulsiveness in food                                                                      |
| **One analysis**                   | 1     | The four elements                                                                          |

The tradition matches subject to temperament (_carita_) rather than treating all forty as interchangeable. The point is to give a particular mind the object that steadies it fastest.

| Temperament      | Tends toward             | Suited object                                  |
| :--------------- | :----------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- |
| **Greedy**       | Grasping at the pleasant | The kinds of foulness; mindfulness of the body |
| **Hating**       | Aversion and irritation  | The four divine abodes; the color kasiṇas      |
| **Deluded**      | Dullness and confusion   | Mindfulness of breathing                       |
| **Faithful**     | Devotion                 | Recollection of the Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha     |
| **Intellectual** | Analysis                 | Recollection of death; the four elements       |
| **Speculative**  | Restless thinking        | Mindfulness of breathing                       |

Not every object reaches the same depth. The recollections and the analytical subjects generally steady the mind to access concentration and no further. Mindfulness of breathing and the kasiṇas carry it through all four form jhānas, and the kasiṇas open into the formless states. Among the divine abodes, loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy reach the third jhāna, and equanimity reaches the fourth.

## Mindfulness of Breathing (_Ānāpānasati_)

Breath is the universal home base. It is always available, it suits both the deluded and the speculative temperaments, and it leads all the way to absorption. The method moves through recognizable stages.

1. **Posture.** Sit upright and settled, with the body relaxed but not slumped. Let the hands rest and the eyes close. Establish an intention to stay with the breath.
2. **Find the touch point.** Bring attention to the place where the breath is felt most clearly, usually the rim of the nostrils or the upper lip. The object is the sensation of breathing at that point, not the idea of the breath and not the movement of the abdomen chased around the body.
3. **Know long and short.** Without controlling the breath, notice when it is long and when it is short. This does nothing but keep attention on the object; the noticing is the practice.
4. **Count, to steady a scattered mind.** If attention keeps slipping, count at the end of each out-breath, one through ten, then start again at one. Lose the count and return to one. Counting is a scaffold, dropped once the mind stays without it.
5. **Follow.** With counting set aside, stay with the breath from beginning to middle to end, a continuous thread of attention rather than a series of checkpoints.
6. **Rest on the sign.** As the mind settles, the breath grows subtle and a mental image, the _nimitta_, appears. Attention shifts from the physical breath to this sign, and holding it steadily is the approach to absorption described below.

## Other Objects

- **Kasiṇas.** Gaze at a device, a disk of earth-colored clay or a color, until the image can be held with the eyes closed as clearly as with them open. That mental image sharpens into the counterpart sign, and fixing on it builds one-pointedness quickly. Kasiṇa practice is the classical route into the formless states.
- **Loving-kindness as a concentration object.** The same mettā radiation developed for ethical cultivation doubles as a concentration subject, warming the mind and carrying it to the third jhāna. The method is on **[SilaTechniques.md](SilaTechniques.md)**; here its function is absorption rather than conduct.
- **Recollection of the Buddha** (_buddhānussati_). Dwelling on the qualities of the Buddha rouses a sluggish or doubting mind. It steadies attention to access concentration and is a common support rather than a route to jhāna.

## The Five Hindrances (_Nīvaraṇa_)

Before the mind can enter the First Jhāna, five conditions must be suppressed. They are called hindrances because they actively block concentration, and each has a working antidote.

| Hindrance                                        | What It Does                           | Antidote                                        |
| :----------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| **Sensuous Lust** (_kāmacchanda_)                | Pulls the mind outward toward pleasure | Contemplate impermanence; guard the sense doors |
| **Ill-Will** (_byāpāda_)                         | Contracts the mind into hostility      | Loving-kindness (_mettā_)                       |
| **Torpor and Languor** (_thīna-middha_)          | Collapses energy; the mind sinks       | Rouse energy; attend to light; adjust posture   |
| **Restlessness and Worry** (_uddhacca-kukkucca_) | Scatters the mind; it cannot settle    | Steady the breath; ground the body; settle Sīla |
| **Skeptical Doubt** (_vicikicchā_)               | Stalls practice through wavering       | Study; clarify the method; recall past progress |

Suppression is not eradication. Concentration holds the hindrances at bay while it lasts; permanent removal is the work of insight (_vipassanā_), covered in **[Panna.md](Panna.md)**.

### The Jhāna Factors as Antidotes

The five factors that define the First Jhāna are not arbitrary. Each one counters a specific hindrance, which is why their arising marks the hindrances' suppression.

| Jhāna Factor                 | Hindrance It Counters                             |
| :--------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| _Vitakka_ (Applied Thought)  | Dullness and drowsiness (it lifts the mind)       |
| _Vicāra_ (Sustained Thought) | Doubt (it holds the mind steady)                  |
| _Pīti_ (Rapture)             | Ill-will (joy excludes resentment)                |
| _Sukha_ (Happiness)          | Restlessness and worry (pleasure calms agitation) |
| _Ekaggata_ (One-Pointedness) | Sensual desire (unification excludes scattering)  |

When a hindrance surfaces during a sit, the corresponding factor has weakened. The remedy is to reconnect with that quality rather than to fight the hindrance directly. Balancing the energy behind these factors is the domain of Right Effort, whose lute simile and five methods for removing distracting thoughts are on **[RightEffort.md](RightEffort.md)**.

## The Approach to Absorption

Concentration deepens through two stages, and the transition between them is marked by how the object appears.

**Access Concentration** (_upacāra-samādhi_) - The hindrances are suppressed and the mind hovers around the object, stable enough to rest there but still prone to drifting, like a child who can walk but still falls. Access is already enough for insight work.

**Absorption Concentration** (_appanā-samādhi_) - The mind is fully immersed in the counterpart sign and no longer needs effort to stay, like a person walking straight and steadily. This is the gateway to the formal jhāna hierarchy in **[SamadhiAttainments.md](SamadhiAttainments.md)**.

The object passes through three signs on the way:

- **Preliminary Sign** - the original physical object, such as the touch of the breath, as perceived through the senses.
- **Learning Sign** (_uggaha-nimitta_) - a stable mental image of the object, as clear as the original was to the senses, held now without sensory input.
- **Counterpart Sign** (_paṭibhāga-nimitta_) - a purified, luminous image arising from the learning sign, compared to the moon emerging from a cloud. Its appearance marks the threshold of access concentration, with the First Jhāna within reach.

---

## The Two Routes

Not every practitioner develops the form jhānas. There are two ways to reach concentration deep enough to support insight.

- **The jhāna route** - Develop full absorption through the stages above, then turn the still, wieldy mind toward the Three Characteristics. The Fourth Jhāna gives the steadiest platform.
- **Momentary Concentration** (_khaṇika-samādhi_) - A mobile unification that stays with the changing stream of phenomena instead of fixing on one stable object. Each arising event becomes the object. It does not produce jhāna, but it delivers enough stillness to serve as the platform for vipassanā, and it is the concentration used on the direct insight path in **[PannaTechniques.md](PannaTechniques.md)**.
